Research Agenda

My research examines how scientific careers, mobility, relational ties, and place jointly shape the production and circulation of knowledge. The empirical focus is scientific and high-skilled mobility: where researchers move, which prior relationships are associated with destination choice, and how mobility may reorganise collaboration and innovation across organisations and cities. The work is quantitative and evidence-led, with careful separation between employment moves, publication affiliations, collaboration ties, and regional innovation outcomes.

01

How do scientists choose destinations?

The first line of work studies career-stage transitions, city opportunity structures, distance, national borders, institutional platforms, and explicit candidate destinations.

02

How do relationships shape mobility?

The second line distinguishes direct coauthor ties, publication-location exposure, ego-network embeddedness, and historical mobility ties before each observed move.

03

How does mobility reorganise knowledge production?

The third line asks whether mobility is followed by tie retention, tie replacement, brokerage, and changes in regional knowledge combinations.

Scientific Careers and Mobility

Research question

How do elite scientists move across organisations and cities over long career histories, and which city-level conditions are associated with mobility ties?

Why it matters

Scientific mobility is one way knowledge, skills, and institutional access circulate across places. Treating moves as temporally verified career events avoids conflating employment, residence, and publication affiliation.

Current empirical work

An accepted first-author article in World Regional Studies examines the spatial structure and influencing factors of global top biomedical scientists’ mobility from an urban-network perspective. The master’s thesis extends this work through whole-network and ego-network analysis.

Data

Career-history records for top biomedical scientists, institution-city linkage, education and employment stages, mobility events, city attributes, and platform histories.

Methods

Career reconstruction, directed weighted mobility networks, social network analysis, core-periphery description, WERGM, GIS interpretation, and mobility ego-network analysis.

Related output

Accepted article · Submitted manuscript

Collaboration Networks and Relational Capital

Research question

Do scientists select destinations where they already have collaboration ties, and how do tie breadth, persistence, timing, and network share differ across candidate cities?

Why it matters

Prior collaboration may provide information, credibility, and access to scientific communities, but observed associations should not be read as evidence of recruitment, job offers, or causal hiring mechanisms.

Current empirical work

A submitted manuscript treats observed intercity employment moves as event-specific choices. It compares selected cities with alternative candidate destinations and measures destination-specific direct coauthor ties before each move.

Data

Verified career histories, Scopus publication records, author and affiliation records, coauthor ties, annual city publication counts, and event-candidate destination structures.

Methods

Event-specific candidate sets, event-stratified conditional logit, clustered robust inference, time-ordered tie measurement, and sensitivity checks.

Related output

Submitted manuscript

Innovation, Knowledge Diffusion, and Regional Opportunity

Research question

How do high-skilled mobility, collaboration networks, and organisational contexts relate to regional knowledge recombination and innovation opportunity?

Why it matters

Regions differ in their capacity to attract, retain, and connect researchers. The current agenda treats regional innovation as an empirical question requiring compatible data on talent flows, knowledge production, firms, and places.

Current empirical work

The current extension links scientific-career evidence to innovation measurement while keeping coverage, ownership, and temporal compatibility explicit. Historical projects include China-U.S. biomedical innovation comparison, firm-year innovation measurement, nighttime-light resilience, and global trade networks.

Data

Linked bibliometric and career records, city innovation attributes, patents, trademarks, R&D expenditure, VIIRS-DNB nighttime lights, UN Comtrade, and policy or planning documents when permitted.

Methods

Measurement audit, entity resolution, panel construction, spatial analysis, network analysis, comparative design, and reproducible Python/R documentation.

Related output

China-U.S. biomedical innovation · Urban resilience · Global trade network

Research Trajectory

01

Spatial and quantitative foundations

Remote sensing, trade networks, and applied modelling

Early work used nighttime-light data, Fisher Information, DTW, K-Means, UN Comtrade, GIS, and social network analysis to build a practical base in spatial and relational data.

GIS · VIIRS-DNB · Fisher Information · DTW · K-Means · UN Comtrade

02

Scientific mobility networks

From career histories to intercity mobility structure

Career histories are matched to institutions and cities, allowing individual moves to be analysed as directed mobility relations among cities.

Career histories · Institution-city linkage · SNA · GIS

03

Relational capital and destination choice

From realised moves to candidate-destination comparison

Observed moves are organised as event-specific choice sets so selected cities can be compared with plausible alternatives through pre-move collaboration ties.

Candidate sets · Conditional logit · Coauthor ties · Temporal ordering

04

Knowledge-network reconfiguration

From mobility events to collaboration and innovation systems

The next step asks whether mobility is followed by tie retention, tie replacement, brokerage, and changes in regional knowledge combinations.
